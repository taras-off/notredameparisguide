Why it matters

Why Visit Notre-Dame de Paris?

On 15 April 2019 the world watched the spire fall. Five years later, on 8 December 2024, the doors opened again — stone cleaned to a colour Paris had not seen in a century, a new oak spire on the skyline, and the great organ back in voice. This is the rare monument you can visit at the exact moment it came back from the dead.

Building started in 1163 and ran for nearly two centuries. Kings were crowned steps away, Napoleon crowned himself here, the Revolution smashed its statues, and Victor Hugo's novel dragged it back from ruin. Eight hundred years of French history happened inside these walls.

Notre-Dame de Paris is one of the first buildings to use the flying buttress — the stone "leg" that props the walls from outside so the inside could open up into glass. Look up: the ceiling is 33 metres high, held by ribs of stone thinner than they have any right to be.

The gargoyles that stare over Paris are younger than they look. The Revolutionaries beheaded a row of stone kings, mistaking them for French royalty — and the heads turned up two centuries later under a Paris bank. The best stories here are the ones the official signs skip.

The Story of Notre-Dame de Paris

A Forest, a Fire, and a Resurrection

The roof had a nickname: la forêt — the forest. It was built from around 1,300 oak trees, some felled in the 1160s, a lattice of medieval beams held up for 800 years. On 15 April 2019 it burned in a few hours. The spire buckled and fell through the vaulted ceiling on live television.

What did not burn is the real story. The stone rib-vaults mostly held. The three great rose windows survived. Firefighters formed a human chain to carry out the Crown of Thorns and the relics. And on the morning after, high on the facade, the golden rooster that had crowned the spire was found in the rubble — dented, but whole.

Five years and hundreds of millions of euros later, the cathedral reopened. The rebuilders chose to make it as it was — the same oak "forest," a faithful copy of the 19th-century spire — so what you walk into today is both brand new and 800 years old.

The Rose Windows: Glass That Survived Everything

Three round windows, three centuries of weather, wars and one fire — and the medieval glass is still there. The south rose is about 13 metres across. Two Paris metro carriages, parked end to end, would fit inside the circle.

Stand under the north rose on a bright afternoon and the blue seems to come from behind the wall. That blue is 13th-century, made with a recipe we still can't fully reproduce.

The Gargoyles That Are Younger Than the United States

The famous horned creatures brooding over the city — the chimères — feel medieval. They aren't. They were added in the 1840s by the architect Viollet-le-Duc, who restored the cathedral and gave it the look most of us picture. They were never meant to scare demons. They were meant to be photographed. It worked.

The Night the Revolution Beheaded the Wrong Kings

Across the west front runs the Gallery of Kings — 28 stone figures. During the Revolution a crowd tore them down, certain they were kings of France. They were actually kings of ancient Judah. The heads vanished. In 1977, workers digging under a Paris bank found 21 of them, buried for nearly two hundred years. You can meet those original heads today in the Musée de Cluny, a few minutes' walk away.

The Book That Saved a Cathedral

By 1830 Notre-Dame was a wreck — crumbling, half-forgotten, nearly torn down. Then Victor Hugo published The Hunchback of Notre-Dame and made the building itself the hero. Paris fell back in love with it. The restoration that followed is the reason it was still standing to be saved in 2019.

Practical info

Visiting Notre-Dame de Paris

Entry to the cathedral is free — there is no ticket to buy. A free timed slot on the official site lets you walk in faster at busy hours. The towers, crypt and treasury are separate, paid visits.

  • EntryCathedral: free · no ticket · free timed reservation recommended
  • HoursOpen daily, roughly 8:00–19:00 (confirm on the official site)
  • Address6 Parvis Notre-Dame – Pl. Jean-Paul II, 75004 Paris
  • MetroCité (line 4) · Saint-Michel–Notre-Dame (RER B/C)
  • Separate & paidArchaeological Crypt · the Towers · the Treasury

How entry works

Free · timed reservation · no ticket to buy

There is no entry ticket for the cathedral, and no official "skip-the-line" pass. Entry is free. Booking a free timed slot on the official Notre-Dame website or app simply lets you walk in faster when it's busy.

  • Reserve a free slot on the official site (recommended)
  • Early morning is the calmest time to walk in
  • Towers, crypt and treasury are separate, paid visits

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Notre-Dame de Paris free to visit?
Yes. Entry to the cathedral is free. A free timed reservation is recommended to avoid the longest lines. Paid options nearby include the Archaeological Crypt, the towers and the Treasury.
Has Notre-Dame de Paris reopened after the fire?
Yes. The cathedral reopened on 8 December 2024, five years after the 2019 fire, fully restored inside with a new oak spire.
How long do you need at Notre-Dame de Paris?
Around one to two hours for the cathedral and the square. The self-guided audio tour runs about two hours across 31 stops, inside and around Île de la Cité, at your own pace.
Is the audio tour available offline?
Yes. Download it once in the TouringBee app and it works fully offline, with no data needed on site. The offline GPS map shows where you are; you start each track yourself at the spot.
What languages is the audio guide in?
Ten: English, French, German, Spanish, Italian, Portuguese, Polish, Russian, Korean and Dutch.
Does it work on iPhone and Android?
Yes, the TouringBee app runs on both iOS and Android. Buy once and keep access for a year.
Can you go up the towers of Notre-Dame?
Tower access is managed separately and is reopening in stages after the restoration. Check current availability before you go; the audio tour covers the towers' story from the ground.
