Is Notre-Dame open again? Yes — and here's what changed

This is the first thing people want to know, and the answer is a clear yes. The cathedral reopened in December 2024, five years and nearly eight months after the fire that destroyed its roof and spire. What visitors notice first isn't the new timberwork hidden above the vault — it's the light. Centuries of soot and candle grime were cleaned from the pale limestone during the restoration, and the interior came back markedly brighter than anyone alive had seen it. The spire is new but identical to the one that fell; the stone is cleaner; the organ, nearly 8,000 pipes, has been rebuilt and plays again. Work continues on parts of the exterior and the forecourt into roughly 2027, but the cathedral itself is fully open. The whole dramatic arc — the night it nearly collapsed, the hour that saved it, and the five-year rebuild — is worth knowing before you go: we tell it in full in the Notre-Dame fire guide.

How to visit: free entry, timed slots, and the queue nobody warns you about

Entry to Notre-Dame is free — it always has been; it's a working cathedral, not a museum. What's new since the reopening is the demand. A free timed reservation, booked online in advance, is strongly recommended and will save you the longest wait; a separate, shorter line exists for people coming to pray. Bags are checked at a security point, large backpacks aren't allowed and there's no cloakroom, and a dress code is enforced at the door — shoulders and knees covered. None of it is complicated once you know it, but it catches a lot of visitors off guard. Our step-by-step guide to visiting Notre-Dame covers reservations, security, the dress code and the etiquette inside a cathedral that's holding Mass while you walk through it.

The best time to go (and the time to avoid)

If you can, go early. The first hour after opening and the last hour before closing are the calmest; the middle of the day, weekends, and the summer peak are the busiest. Season matters too — a weekday in November is a completely different experience from a Saturday in July. Light matters as well: the south rose window is at its best when the afternoon sun is behind it. We break down the quietest hours, days and months, and the best light for photos, in the best time to visit guide.

Tickets: what's free, and what you actually pay for

Here's the part that confuses people. The cathedral itself is free. What costs money are the extras: climbing the towers (the famous view, up among the gargoyles — reserved separately and in high demand), the archaeological crypt under the square, and the Treasury with its relics and reliquaries. Guided tours and, of course, the audio guide are separate again. If you want to know exactly what each one costs, what's worth it, and what you can skip, our tickets guide lays it all out.

What to see outside: a stone bible on the facade

Most people walk straight up to the doors and miss the best lesson Notre-Dame offers — the one carved across its front. The west facade is a book in stone: the gallery of twenty-eight kings across the middle, the three great portals carved like a medieval comic strip, the central rose window, and the two towers framing it all. For a city that was largely illiterate when this was built, the sculpture was the text — the "poor man's bible." Learn to read it and the facade stops being decoration and becomes a set of stories. And then there are the famous grotesques high on the towers — which raises a question most visitors get wrong: the difference between a gargoyle and a chimera. (One is plumbing; the other is pure 19th-century imagination. The audio guide tells you which is which, and where to look.)

What to see inside: rose windows, the organ, and the relics

Inside, three things stop almost everyone. The three rose windows — the oldest glass dating to the 13th century, and all three survived the fire — glow like walls of jewels when the light is right. The great organ, one of the largest in France, survived the flames and was painstakingly cleaned of lead dust, pipe by pipe. And the cathedral's relics, including the Crown of Thorns, carried out of the burning building by a human chain in 2019, are kept in the Treasury. Beyond the headline sights there are chapels, tombs, a 14th-century choir screen carved with the life of Christ, and the famous statue of the Virgin of Paris. Knowing what you're looking at — and why it matters — is the whole difference between walking past and actually seeing it.

The fire that nearly ended it — and the rebuild that saved it

You can't understand today's Notre-Dame without the night of April 15, 2019. The roof and spire burned and collapsed, but the stone vault beneath held like a shield, the towers were saved by firefighters who went up into the flames, and a chain of people carried the relics to safety. Then came a five-year rebuild, €840 million in donations from 340,000 people across 150 countries, 2,000 oak trees for a new medieval-style frame, and a national argument over whether to rebuild it exactly as it was or add a modern touch. (They chose "à l'identique" — identical — with one small modern concession.) It's one of the great stories of our time, and we tell all of it — what was lost, what survived by luck, and how it came back — in the fire guide.

The stories most visitors never hear

This is where a visit tips from "nice" to unforgettable. Standing on the square, you're on ground that has staged two thousand years of Paris — and the cathedral has seen things that sound invented:

  • A king of England was once crowned under this roof — and there's a reason it could happen only once.
  • A royal groom married here but, because of his faith, wasn't allowed inside for his own wedding.
  • There's a copper marker on the square — the official centre of France — with a small ritual that's said to bring you back to Paris.
  • A vanished "Court of Miracles," the lawless beggars' quarter Victor Hugo made famous, once sat in these streets.
  • One statue on the facade carries its own head in its hands — and the saint behind that very Parisian image.

We don't spoil the endings here — they're best heard on the spot, in front of the exact stone they belong to. A short history of Notre-Dame, in five minutes

Construction began in 1163 under Bishop Maurice de Sully, on the site of earlier churches, and the main structure took the better part of two centuries. It was a radical building for its day — one of the first to use flying buttresses, which let the walls rise higher and open up for huge windows. It crowned a French king's story more than once, was ransacked and rededicated to "Reason" during the Revolution, and then fell into such neglect that demolition was seriously discussed. What saved it was a novel: Victor Hugo's The Hunchback of Notre-Dame (1831) made the public fall back in love with the cathedral, and a major 19th-century restoration by Eugène Viollet-le-Duc followed — he designed the spire that would later burn. Napoleon crowned himself Emperor here in 1804. The 2019 fire and the 2024 reopening are simply the latest chapters in a very long story. The island it stands on: Île de la Cité

Notre-Dame doesn't stand alone — it sits at one end of the Île de la Cité, the island in the Seine where Paris itself began as a Roman town called Lutetia. Steps away are the dazzling stained glass of Sainte-Chapelle, the former royal palace and prison of the Conciergerie, the flower market, and the quiet tip of the island where locals picnic. If you've come all this way, the cathedral is really the centrepiece of a half-day on the island — we map the whole walk, story by story, in the Île de la Cité walking guide.

Frequently asked questions

Is Notre-Dame open to visitors in 2026?
Yes. The cathedral reopened on December 8, 2024, and is fully open. Entry is free; a free timed reservation is recommended. Work on parts of the exterior and surroundings continues into about 2027.

How much does it cost to visit?
Entering the cathedral is free. The towers, the archaeological crypt and the Treasury have separate tickets, and guided tours and the audio guide are extra. See our tickets guide for current prices.

How long should I spend there?
Plan on about two hours to do it justice, inside and out. A quick look is 20–30 minutes; with an audio guide, around two hours.

Can you climb the towers?
The towers reopened in stages after the restoration and are visited on a separate, timed ticket that sells out quickly. Details and booking advice are in the tickets guide.

What's the best time to avoid the crowds?
Early morning or late afternoon, midweek, outside the summer peak. Our best time to visit guide has the details by hour, day and season.
